
This RMO problem <span style="color: var(--lColorAc);">($\textit{2017, Q.2.}$)</span> uses remainders to easily prove no solutions exist for a given diophantine.

<div style="border: 1px solid var(--lColorAc); padding: 12px; border-radius: 4px; margin-top: 12px;">

Show that the equation

$$\small a^3 + (a+1)^3 + (a+2)^3 + (a+3)^3 + (a+4)^3 + (a+5)^3 + (a+6)^3 = b^4 + (b+1)^4$$

has no solutions in integers $a$, $b$.
</div>

<br>

<div style="text-align: justify; width: 90%;margin: 0 auto;">

As soon as we see the equation, the first observation that we are able to make is that the left-hand side consists of cubes of $7$ consecutive integers. We observe that seven consecutive integers have distinct remainders when divided by $7$. Therefore taking $\bmod$ $7$ seems to helpful.

<br>

For the sake of contradiction we assume that the equation has solutions for some $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$

<br>

As all $a$, $a+1$, ... , $a+6$ have distinct remainders we can assume WLOG $a = 7k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$

<br>

Consequently $a + 1 = 7k + 1$, $a + 2 = 7k + 2$ and so on.

<br>

We denote $a = a_0$ , $a+1 = a_1$ , ... , $a+6 = a_6$

<br>

Therefore the following statements are true:

$$a_0 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$$

$$\Rightarrow  a_0^3 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_1^3 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_2^3 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_3^3 \equiv 6 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_4^3 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_5^3 \equiv 6 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_6^3 \equiv 6 \pmod{7}$$

On summing all the above relations we get,

$$\sum_{i=0}^{6} a_i^3 \equiv 21 \pmod{7}$$

$$\Rightarrow \sum_{i=0}^{6} a_i^3 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$$

This shows that the left-hand side of equation is divisible by $7$. Now we check for the right-hand side. As per our assumption RHS should also be divisible by $7$.

We use previously defined $a_i$ to find remainders for fourth powers of seven consecutive integers.

<br>

$$a_0^4 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_1^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_2^4 \equiv 2 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_3^4 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_4^4 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_5^4 \equiv 2 \pmod{7}$$

$$a_6^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$$

<br>

Hence, the remainders of $b^4$ and $(b+1)^4$ when divided by $7$ correspond to one of the following tuples: $(0,1), (1,2), (2,4), (4,4), (4,2), (2,1),(1,0)$

<br>

Therefore the remainder when RHS is divided by $7$ will be the sum of elements in the tuple. For $(0,1)$ it is $1$, for $(1,2)$ it is $3$, for $(2,4)$ it is $6$, for $(4,4)$ it is $8$ which is the same as $1$. Similar can be done for subsequent tuples.

<br>

So, if $\text{RHS} \equiv x \pmod{7}$, then $x \in \\{1, 3, 6\\}$.


<br>

Hence, LHS is divisible by $7$ and RHS is not divisible by $7$ which is a contradiction. Therefore the equation has no solutions for integers $a$ and $b. \hspace{2.5cm} \square$


</div>
