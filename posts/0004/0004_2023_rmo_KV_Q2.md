
This RMO problem <span style="color: var(--lColorAc);">($\textit{2023, KV JNV, Q.2.}$)</span> uses remainders and observation of equations to prove divisibility of a number by $36$.
<div style="border: 1px solid var(--lColorAc); padding: 12px; border-radius: 4px; margin-top: 12px;">

Given a prime number $p$ such that the number $2p$ is equal to the sum of squares of some four consecutive positive integers. Prove that $p-7$ is divisible by $36$.
</div>

<br>

<div style="text-align: justify; width: 90%;margin: 0 auto;">

Let us assume the four consecutive positive integers to be $(x-1)$, $x$, $(x+1)$ and $(x+2)$.

<br>

Therefore,

<br>

$$2p = (x-1)^2 + x^2 + (x+1)^2 + (x+2)^2$$
$$\Rightarrow 2p = x^2 - 2x + 1 + x^2 + x^2 + 2x + 1 + x^2 + 4x + 4$$
$$\Rightarrow 2p = 4x^2 + 4x + 6$$
$$\Rightarrow p = 2x^2 + 2x + 3 \tag{1}$$
$$\Rightarrow p - 7 = 2x^2 + 2x - 4$$
$$\Rightarrow p - 7 = 2(x+2)(x-1) \tag{2}$$

In $(1)$ on putting $x=2$ (which is the minimum value for all four consecutive integers to be positive), we get $p \ge 15$.

<br>
On observing RHS of $(2)$ we see that $(x+2)$ and $(x-1)$ have opposite parity, therefore one of them must be even and the other must be odd. Hence, $p-7$ is divisible by $4$.

<br>

A prime number greater than $3$ can only be of the form $6k \pm 1$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

<br>

As $p \ge 15 > 3$ we can take $p =  6k + 1$ or $p = 6k - 1$.  If $ p = 6k + 1$ then by $(2)$,

$$ 6k - 6 = 2(x+2)(x-1)$$
$$\Rightarrow 3(k-1) = (x+2)(x-1)$$

Therefore as the LHS is divisble by $3$, the RHS must also be divisible by $3$.

<br>

So, $3 \mid (x+2)$ or $3 \mid (x-1)$, but $(x+2)-(x-1)=3$. Hence if one of $(x+2)$ and $(x-1)$ is divisible by $3$, then both must be divisible by $3$. This implies that $(x+2)(x-1)$ is divisible by $9$, therefore $p-7$ is divisible by $9$.

<br>

We have already shown that $p-7$ is divisible by $4$ and as $\gcd(9,4) = 1$, implies:

<br>

$p-7$ is divisible by $36$.

<br>

<div style="border: 1px solid var(--lColorAc); padding: 12px; border-radius: 4px; margin-top: 12px;">
Now we evaluate the case wherein $p = 6k - 1$. By $(1)$ we get,
</div>

<br>

$$6k - 1 = 2x^2 + 2x + 3$$
$$\Rightarrow 3k = x^2 + x + 2$$

For this to be true, RHS must be divisible by 3. We take three cases as follows,

<br>

**Case (a) :** $  x \equiv 0\pmod{3}$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 \equiv 0\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x \equiv 0\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x + 2 \equiv 2\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow RHS \equiv 2\pmod{3}$$
but $LHS \equiv 0\pmod{3}$ therefore this case is not possible.

<br>

**Case (b) :** $  x \equiv 1\pmod{3}$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 \equiv 1\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x \equiv 2\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x + 2 \equiv 4\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x + 2 \equiv 1\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow RHS \equiv 1\pmod{3}$$
but $LHS \equiv 0\pmod{3}$ therefore this case is also not possible.

<br>

**Case (c) :** $  x \equiv 2\pmod{3}$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 \equiv 4\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 \equiv 1\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x \equiv 3\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x + 2 \equiv 5\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow x^2 + x + 2 \equiv 2\pmod{3}$$
$$\Rightarrow RHS \equiv 2\pmod{3}$$
but $LHS \equiv 0\pmod{3}$ therefore this case is also not possible.

<br>
Hence, we can entirely reject the possibility of $p = 6k - 1$ and only $p = 6k + 1$ holds.$\hspace{10.5cm} \square$

</div>
